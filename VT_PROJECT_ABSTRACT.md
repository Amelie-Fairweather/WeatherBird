# WeatherBird: Real-Time Road Safety Assessment System for Vermont

## Abstract

As recent as 2021, extreme flooding in Vermont was responsible for 49.4 million dollars in reparations and damage to over 4,000 homes. Living in the small, rural town of Charlotte, one of my biggest fears is the commute from my house to school every morning during the winter. To address this critical public safety need, I developed WeatherBird, an automated road safety assessment system that aggregates real-time weather and road condition data from multiple sources to provide accurate safety ratings for Vermont roads. The hypothesis was that multi-source data aggregation would significantly improve accuracy compared to single-source systems, and that weather-based predictions could serve as a reliable fallback when direct road condition data is unavailable. The methodology involved integrating six independent weather APIs (NWS, Weatherbit, Weatherstack, Visual Crossing, OpenWeatherMap, Xweather) and seven road condition sources (VTrans RWIS, NWS alerts, TomTom Traffic, VTrans Lane Closures, VTrans Traffic Incidents, Xweather Road API, New England 511). A multi-factor scoring algorithm was developed that analyzes temperature (20% weight), road surface conditions (40% weight), data freshness (10% weight), severity levels (15% weight), source reliability (5% weight), road type (5% weight), time of day (3% weight), and combination effects (2% weight) to calculate safety scores from 0-100. The system processes road condition data for all major Vermont highways and 11 key locations. Multi-source aggregation provides more reliable assessments than single-source systems by cross-validating data across multiple APIs and prioritizing government sources (VTrans RWIS, NWS) which have higher reliability scores. Temperature analysis shows strong correlation with dangerous road conditions, as colder temperatures (≤32°F) significantly increase ice formation risk. The weather-based fallback system ensures 100% uptime even when primary data sources fail, allowing the system to generate road condition predictions from weather data when direct road sensors are unavailable. The hypothesis was supported: multi-source data aggregation provides more reliable assessments by cross-validating data across sources and prioritizing high-reliability government sources, and weather-based predictions provide critical fallback ensuring system reliability. The system successfully processes real-time data from multiple sources and provides actionable safety assessments for Vermont roads. However, the system has limitations including data freshness constraints, geographic coverage gaps for rural roads, and dependency on external APIs. Future research directions include machine learning models for predictive analytics, integration with IoT road sensors, and expansion to other states. This system demonstrates that automated road safety assessment is feasible and accurate, providing Vermont residents with real-time information to make safer travel decisions during adverse weather conditions.
